Chester: [serious] Right. FOM 5.1.8.1, the APU. Here's the testable bit, word for word: an APU is considered to have started successfully on any one of three start attempts. Pualani: [excited] Ooh — that's memory aid number one, right out of the gate. "APU gets three swings." One good start out of three, you're golden. Three consecutive whiffs, it failed, and you write it up in the Maintenance Logbook.

Chester: [warm] And a neighbor to that one, FOM 5.1.7 — doors. Doors shall be armed for any surface movement, including all pushbacks, taxi, and towing. Any surface movement. Not just the takeoff roll.

Otto: [serious] That's a good one to tattoo on the inside of your eyelids. If the airplane is rolling on the ground with people on board, those doors are armed. Full stop. And you make sure the flight attendants have adequate time and notice to secure the cabin before you move.

Pualani: [warm] While we're in the 5.1 general section — the crew rest facility, FOM 5.1.23.1. On the 787 that's the Overhead Flight Crew Rest. The rule: it must not be occupied from pushback to 10,000 feet AFE, and from 45 minutes prior to landing to aircraft block-in.

Otto: [dry] So the window is: wheels-up-ish to forty-five-out. Bookended. Kind of like this podcast — nothing of value happens at the start or the end.

Chester: [laughs] Rude, and accurate.

Chester: [warm] One more general-section item that is pure widebody — FOM 5.1.3, narrow runways. A narrow runway is a nominal paved width less than 100 feet — that's 30 meters. And on the 787, takeoff and landing are not authorized on any narrow runway. Period.

Pualani: [excited] "Under a hundred, we don't run it." The 787 needs its elbow room.

Otto: [dry] Less than a hundred feet wide, the widebody stays home and pouts. I respect it. I too refuse to operate in tight spaces.

Chester: [warm] Before we push, one for the fancy-approach nerds, since it lives up here in the 5.1 section — RNP AR, FOM 5.1.4.1. These look like RNAV GPS approaches but with two notable differences: RNP values of 0.3 or less, and Radius to Fix legs — the RF legs. Both the crew and the aircraft have to be certified for them.

Pualani: [serious] And the prohibition, FOM 5.1.4.3 — the use of V/S mode on the final approach is prohibited. So on those, no vertical-speed mode down final. Keep your paws off it.

Otto: [dry] Point-three or less, and curved RF legs. If it's got a curve and a tiny number, treat it like it's radioactive. And no V/S. We clear? Sweet.

Chester: [warm] Alright. Push and start's done, doors armed, APU behaved on swing number two. Let's taxi. FOM 5.3.1, short and absolute: taxi operations are prohibited when braking action is reported as NIL by the airport or another aircraft. NIL braking, you do not taxi. Not "gently." Not at all.

Pualani: [serious] And the callout culture — FOM 5.3.4.1, runway awareness. Dual acknowledgment is required for five clearances. You ready? Runway Crossing, Hold Short, Line Up and Wait, Cleared for Takeoff, and Cleared to Land.

Otto: [dry] Five. Both pilots say it out loud. It's the one corner of aviation where repeating yourself is not annoying, it's mandatory. My family should be so lucky.

Chester: [warm] And here comes a Top Gun moment — FOM 5.3.11, low visibility taxi. Restricted visibility includes dense fog, heavy precipitation, or any time the RVR is less than 1200. And the rule is explicit: during low visibility operations, the Captain must taxi the aircraft.

Pualani: [excited] So no, Maverick, you don't get to buzz the tower, and you don't get to taxi in the fog. Below the numbers, that's the Captain's airplane.

Otto: [dry] Chester's been holding that Maverick line since Monday. I watched it approach from ten miles out — which, ironically, is a lot better than 1200 RVR.

Chester: [laughs] Guilty as charged. And Pua, tell them what you do when you actually can't see, because the FOM is specific.

Pualani: [serious] Right — when taxi visibility is marginal, you perform checklists with the aircraft stopped and the parking brake set. You devote maximum attention to scanning outside, all exterior lights on except landing lights, and you do not perform any duties unrelated to taxiing until you're stopped with the brake set. That's the whole low-vis taxi discipline in one breath.

Otto: [dry] Story time, actually. Chester, you want to tell the one about the FO who tried to run the taxi checklist while rolling in the fog up at Anchorage?

Chester: [warm] Oh, tell me about a time. So — years ago, ANC, dead of winter, ice fog rolled in off the inlet thick as chowder. RVR's sitting around nine hundred. Stopped the airplane. Set the park brake. And I said, look: FOM 5.3.11, below twelve hundred RVR that's restricted visibility, checklists happen stopped, brake set, eyes outside. The airplane doesn't care how fast your flow is if you drive it into a taxi sign in Anchorage in January.

Otto: [serious] And that's the IOE lesson right there. The manual isn't punishing you. It's the accumulated scar tissue of everyone who tried it the other way.

Pualani: [warm] IOE gold. Okay — takeoff. This is where the numbers get dense, so buckle in.

Pualani: [serious] FOM 5.4.1.1 — standard takeoff minimums. Defined as 1 statute mile visibility or RVR 5000 for aircraft having two engines or less. The 787 has two engines, so that's us.

Chester: [serious] Then FOM 5.4.3, Captain required takeoff. Any time the visibility is less than or equal to one-quarter statute mile or 1600 RVR, the Captain makes the takeoff.

Otto: [dry] Quarter mile or sixteen hundred. Below that, it's the left seat's leg. Every time. No debate, no coin flip.

Pualani: [excited] Time for memory aid number two, and this one's the fleet number everybody has to carry. Takeoff alternate distance, FOM 5.4.2 — for the 787, it's 393 nautical miles.

Chester: [warm] And how do we lock in 393 without inventing a number out of thin air?

Pualani: [excited] "Three-nine-three, that's the seven-eight-three, for me." It rhymes if you don't examine it too closely. 787, takeoff alternate, 393 nautical miles. And that alternate's weather has to meet the same requirements as a destination alternate — it's not a lower bar just because it's up front.

Otto: [dry] It does not rhyme. It commits crimes against rhyme. But I will now never forget it, and I resent you for that.

Chester: [laughs] Effective mnemonics are annoying by design. Okay — the rejected takeoff, FOM 5.4.10. This one is 787-specific in the number that matters, and it comes up on every checkride.

Otto: [serious] The decision to reject the takeoff shall be the Captain's. Once the command has been given to reject, the takeoff will not be continued. On the 787, an RTO initiated at greater than 80 knots requires that the flight returns to the gate and a Maintenance Logbook entry be made — regardless of the reason.

Pualani: [serious] At or below 80 knots for something correctable, you might not have to go back. But above 80? "Eighty and back to the gate." And a safety report after any RTO, high speed or low.

Chester: [warm] And even below 80, you consider total brake temperature limits before another takeoff. If brake energy is a concern, you write it up with the aircraft weight, reject speed, and type of braking used.

Otto: [dry] Simple version: you call stopping, you're stopping. No takebacks, no "actually let's keep going." The word "stopping" is a one-way door. Unlike me, who is a two-way valve.

Chester: [laughs] There it is.

Pualani: [excited] Okay, we survived the runway. We're airborne. Turns after takeoff, FOM 5.5.1. When there's no procedurally defined turn point, the initial turn is normally commenced at a minimum of 400 feet AFE — to comply with TERPS obstacle criteria.

Chester: [serious] With one exception: a required course or heading change of less than 15 degrees initially is considered a non-maneuvering turn by TERPS, and can be commenced below 400 feet.

Otto: [dry] Under fifteen degrees, it barely counts as a turn. It's a lane change. You don't even signal.

Chester: [warm] Now, cruise. And this is altimetry, so Otto, this is your Super Bowl. RVSM.

Otto: [dry] I'm always awake. My eyes are merely resting. Because I am the autopilot, and I have the aircraft.

Pualani: [laughs] Of course you do. Okay — FOM 5.5.6.5, enroute RVSM altitude monitoring. At initial level-off you verify all altimeters are set to 29.92 and crosscheck them. The two primary altimeters should agree within 200 feet, or a lesser value if the FCOM specifies. And you crosscheck against the standby about every hour.

Chester: [serious] And the airspace itself, FOM 5.5.6.2 — RVSM is required FL290 to FL410, inclusive, with 1000 feet of vertical separation, in all the airspace we're authorized for, including oceanic. And using the standby altimeter as a primary is prohibited in RVSM airspace.

Otto: [serious] Two hundred feet between the primaries. That's the number to carry. Bigger gap than that, something is wrong and you owe it a look.

Chester: [warm] Which is the perfect setup — because the FOM has a whole failure branch, FOM 5.5.6.4. In the event of required RVSM equipment failure, prior to or within RVSM airspace, you notify ATC and Dispatch to coordinate a prudent course of action. ATC may not require anything, or you may be looking at a reroute or a diversion.

Pualani: [excited] And Otto, tell me about a time — because I know you've got the oceanic altimeter-split story from the Haneda run, and it's a good one.

Otto: [dry] I do. Honolulu to Haneda. FL350, middle of the Pacific, middle of the night, black as the inside of a cow. Quiet flight. Out over the ocean on the tracks, that is not a "reset it and hum a song" moment.

Otto: [serious] So we did what 5.5.6.4 says. Notified ATC, notified Dispatch, coordinated a prudent course of action. So no quietly promoting the backup and pretending you've got three good boxes.

Pualani: [excited] And it turned out you'd nudged off your assigned altitude, yeah?

Otto: [dry] A hair. Caught an assigned altitude deviation climbing toward 300 feet before it got there.

Chester: [serious] And that flows straight into FOM 5.5.7, altitude deviation reporting. Crew safety report within 24 hours for three things: an assigned altitude deviation of 300 feet or more, an altimeter system error of 245 feet or more, or a total vertical error of 300 feet or more.

Pualani: [serious] "Three, two-forty-five, three — report in twenty-four." Memory aid, no charge.

Chester: [warm] And separately, the Company reports altitude-keeping errors to the FAA within 72 hours. So don't blend the two: crew report, 24 hours. Company report, 72.

If the Captain or First Officer leaves their duty station while the aircraft is above FL410, the remaining crewmember must put on and use their oxygen mask before the other leaves, and keep it on until they return.

Otto: [dry] Above four-one-zero, one pilot up and walking means the other's on the mask. Non-negotiable. It is not a suggestion, it is not "if you feel like it."

Pualani: [excited] And FOM 5.5.13, enroute nav. The 787 is approved for RNP-4 operations. Pualani: [serious] FOM 5.6.5, stabilized approach. For the 787 — normally the aircraft must be aligned with the runway by 500 feet AFE. There's a 300-foot AFE alignment exception at certain airports, and where it exists, it's called out in that airport's 10-7.

Chester: [serious] And the warning in the book is one sentence, all caps in spirit: do not land from an unstable approach. Either crewmember may call for a go-around under the no-fault go-around policy.

Otto: [dry] "Fifteen hundred and five, or a thousand and three with a PAPI." And LAHSO on a wet runway is flat prohibited. That one's easy — wet means no. Calm tailwind, under three knots, is the new number to carry.
